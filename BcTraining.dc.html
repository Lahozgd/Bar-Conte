Method · vermouth

The three-axis ladder

Thirty-six vermouths will never be learned as thirty-six descriptions — they read too much alike. Locate every bottle on three axes instead, then give it one sentence nobody else can claim. Fifteen minutes before service, four steps.

Axis 1 · House

Martini, Cinzano, Carpano, Cocchi, Mancino, Oscar 697, del Professore, Bordiga, Starlino, Chazalettes, Naturale, Stellare. Twelve names, not thirty-six.

Axis 2 · Style

Rosso, Bianco, Ambrato, Dry/Secco, Chinato. Five slots. Every house fills two or three of them and no more.

Axis 3 · Register

Standard or reserve. Riserva Speciale, 1757, Antica Formula, Excelsior, Superiore, Barolo Chinato — the reserve word is on the label.

01

Draw the grid blind

Houses down the side, the five styles across the top. Fill it from memory in three minutes, then walk the shelf and correct it. The holes in your grid are your study list.

02

Blind three of a kind

Same style, three houses, 10 ml each, no labels — three rossos, or three dry. Don't name them; rank them. Sweetest to driest, lightest to heaviest. Ranking sticks where tasting notes don't.

03

One claim each

Every bottle gets a single sentence no other bottle can say. The coffee one. The Barolo one. The Sicilian one. The 1786 one. If two bottles share a sentence, you don't know them apart yet — go find the label detail that splits them.

04

The swap test

A guest orders a Negroni. Name two rossos you could pour instead and say in one sentence what changes in the glass. This is the only part of the job the guest actually hears, so it is the part worth drilling last and hardest.
